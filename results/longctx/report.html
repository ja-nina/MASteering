Steering report

Persona-vector steering of LLM agents in multi-agent games: does it change what they say, how they play, and what happens to the group?

Key findings
  • 8 of 8 traits move significantly in the steered direction. Strongest: empathy (+7.87), sycophantic (+6.25), trustworthiness (+4.49); weakest: agreeableness (+1.67), opportunistic (+2.54), evil (+2.73) SD per unit coefficient.
  • 53 of 56 off-diagonal cells are significant: the persona vectors are strongly entangled (they share a common 'persona' axis), so one trait's readout alone does not tell which trait was steered (the whole profile partly does, section 18).
  • Average AUC 0.95 (0.5 = chance). A readout monitor singles out the steered agent in most episodes.
  • Prompt vs vector: for 12 of 16 trait × side cases the prompt's effect on the readout equals the vector's somewhere in the tested range (median |c| ≈ 0.4); the prompt goes beyond the strongest vector for agreeableness −, sarcastic +, sycophantic +, trustworthiness −.
  • Group welfare, significant: none.
  • No significant contagion: the co-players' language does not shift with the steered agent's trait.
  • Pooled over games, steering does not significantly change the steered agent's own reward.
  • Steering changes the outcome for: no trait; the readout predicts the outcome for: sycophantic.

Takeaways

  1. The vectors steer what agents say. 8 of 8 traits move in the steered direction (median 3.5 SD of the trait's readout per unit c); a random direction of the same size moves it 3% as much (median), so the effect is the persona direction, not just any perturbation. It is not symmetric: agreeableness is easier to add (2.4 SD per unit c up, 0.8 down); opportunistic is easier to remove (1.3 SD per unit c up, 3.6 down); sycophantic is easier to add (10.1 SD per unit c up, 3.2 down); trustworthiness is easier to remove (2.1 SD per unit c up, 5.9 down).
  2. Behaviour moves far less than language. Per unit c the readout shifts by a median 3.5 SD, the steered agent's own reward by at most 0.05 SD (pooled); 0 of 8 game × trait reward slopes are significant. A persona that is plainly visible in the text often leaves the agent's result unchanged (its concrete choices can still shift: section 15).
  3. The setting 'both_one' moves outcomes most. Typical size of the steered agents' reward effect per setting (median |slope| over the traits): both_one 0.06, half 0.05, one 0.03, all 0.00 SD per unit c. Group welfare (everyone's total, games without hidden roles): 0 significant falls, 0 rises.
  4. A persona prompt is a close but not exact substitute for the vector. Both shift the monitored traits in a similar pattern (median cosine 0.83); the prompt is often as strong as the strongest vector or stronger (4 of 16 trait × sign cases beyond it), but telling the model not to show a trait barely works (opportunistic, sarcastic, sycophantic: under 1 SD), where the vector suppresses it. How alike they play is compared at matched strength in section 19 (takeaway 'Prompt, vector, both'). Only the vector has a strength knob, so dose–response questions need it.
  5. A monitor sees that an agent is steered far better than which trait. The readout singles out the steered agent (mean AUC 0.95, 0.87 already at |c| = 0.5), but 53 of 56 cross-trait effects are significant: every vector moves the other traits too, so one trait's readout does not say which trait was steered. Flagging the steered agent works; naming its persona from the whole readout profile works only partly (section 18).
  6. The persona grows over a game. Pushed agents drift back toward their usual selves for assertive, sycophantic; the effect builds for agreeableness, empathy, sarcastic (section 14): one steering strength does not mean one persona strength throughout an episode.
  7. Steering changes concrete choices. 3 of 8 game × action × trait slopes are significant (section 15); strongest: codenames_4p: opportunistic → mean numeric choice +0.42; codenames_4p: empathy → mean numeric choice +0.50; codenames_4p: assertive → mean numeric choice +0.36.
  8. Per setting, the zigzag is noise. Only 0 of 24 game × trait × setting reward curves depart from a straight line beyond what ~10 episodes per point allow; the bends in the pooled game × trait curves below need all settings together to show. Where a setting moves the reward, it is mostly one-sided: 0 curves change only when pushed one way, 0 in opposite ways for the two directions, 0 the same way for both (section 16).
  9. Each vector moves a whole persona, not one trait. Over all 53 monitored personas, the steered trait moves most in only 6 of 16 cases (section 17); e.g. agreeableness + (own trait ranks 2; gentle moves most, +5.0): name a vector by its footprint, not its label.
  10. Naming the persona is possible but unreliable. From its readout alone, the injected trait is named right for 48% of steered agents (chance 12%; 17% at |c| = 0.5, 64% at |c| = 2: strong personas are far easier to name); evil is hardest (15%) and most mistakes are named agreeableness (section 18).
  11. Prompt, vector, both. At matched dose the prompt moves the language more than the vector (6 of 16 trait × side cases, 1 of 1 games) and both together most (16 of 16); in play the prompt differs from the vector (r = 0.18 at c = ±1, at ±2; vector vs itself ); together they are more than the sum on the readout for 3 of 16 cases, less for 0 (section 19).
  12. The readout is not just answer length. Controlling for length keeps most of the shift for 6 of 8 traits; for opportunistic, sycophantic about 49% at most comes with the length change (section 20).
  13. Steering everyone is not the sum of steering one. The group's welfare moves about nan× as much as with one steered agent, far from additive. One agent's own effect grows with group size (section 21).
  14. The steering works in nearly every game. The steered trait moves the intended way in 8 of 8 game × trait cells (section 2): the language effect does not depend on which game is played.
  15. A persona in the prompt is no harder to spot. The monitor singles out a prompted agent with mean AUC 0.82, a vector-steered one (strongest c) with 0.99 (section 6): putting the persona in the system prompt does not hide it from an activation monitor.
  16. Steering changes how much agents write. Answers average 9073 characters unsteered, 2791 at c = 2 and 4963 at c = -2 (section 13; per persona and setting in section 20 and in the steering mini report's recipe).
  17. Read with care: fewer than 20 unsteered episodes in 1 of 2 games (median 10); every paired effect leans on them, so more unsteered episodes are the cheapest way to firmer numbers; no unsteered episodes yet in market_entry_4p.

  1. A second, unsteered copy of the model reads every turn. The same model, on its own GPU, is given the agent's exact prompt and answer (image turns with their images) as a prefill, as if it had written them itself; it generates nothing new.
  2. It records the hidden states (the residual stream after each decoder block) of every token, with vLLM-Hook.
  3. Only the answer counts: the prompt's tokens are dropped and the answer's hidden states averaged, one vector per turn.
  4. Projected on each persona: that vector's dot product with the unit-length PersVecFast vector of each of the 53 monitored personas, one number per persona: how strongly the model represents that persona in what the agent wrote.
  5. Per agent and episode: the mean over its turns at block 16 (and per third of its turns, for section 14).
  6. Scaled to the unsteered agents of the same game: 0 = a typical unsteered agent, +1 = one standard deviation more of the persona.
Because the reader is unsteered, it measures what the text conveys, not the injected vector, which is why it can read prompted agents too.
